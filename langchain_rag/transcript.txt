Artificial intelligence, at its core, is the effort to build machines that can perform tasks that would normally require human intelligence. That includes recognizing images, understanding language, making decisions, and even generating creative work. The field goes back to the nineteen fifties, when researchers gathered at Dartmouth College for a summer workshop that is often considered the birthplace of the term artificial intelligence.

[pause for questions]

In those early days, researchers believed a machine as intelligent as a human was just a decade or two away. They built programs that could prove mathematical theorems, play checkers, and solve simple word problems. But the computers of the time were slow, memory was tiny, and the problems were far more complex than anyone appreciated. This led to what we now call the first AI winter, a period in the nineteen seventies when funding dried up.

[audience laughter]

Machine learning, a subset of AI, enables systems to learn from data without being explicitly programmed. Instead of writing rules by hand, you give the machine examples and let it find patterns on its own. In the nineteen eighties, backpropagation made it practical to train neural networks with multiple layers, loosely inspired by the human brain.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

Deep learning uses neural networks with many layers to model complex patterns. In an image recognition network, early layers detect edges, later layers detect shapes and parts, and final layers recognize whole objects. This hierarchy of learned features is what makes deep learning so powerful, and it generalizes far beyond images, into language, audio, and structured data.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

DeepMind is a British AI research lab founded in twenty ten and acquired by Google in twenty fourteen. It was founded by Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg, and Mustafa Suleyman. Demis Hassabis is the co founder and chief executive officer of DeepMind. He was a child chess prodigy, then a video game designer, then a neuroscientist, before turning to artificial intelligence, with the stated mission to solve intelligence and use it to solve everything else.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

DeepMind is known for breakthroughs like AlphaGo, which defeated the world champion at the board game Go. Go has more possible positions than atoms in the observable universe, and experts thought a machine beating a top human was a decade away. In twenty sixteen, AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol in a five game match watched by millions. Move thirty seven in the second game was so unexpected that commentators initially thought it was a mistake.

[pause for questions]

After AlphaGo came AlphaZero, which taught itself Go, chess, and shogi from scratch with no human games, purely through self play. Then came AlphaFold, which predicts the three dimensional structure of proteins from their amino acid sequence, cracking a fifty year old open problem in biology. DeepMind released predicted structures for nearly every known protein, free, to the scientific community.

Large language models are trained on vast amounts of text and can generate human like responses. These models are built on the transformer architecture, introduced in a twenty seventeen paper titled Attention Is All You Need. The key innovation is attention, letting the model weigh the importance of different words relative to each other, regardless of distance in the sentence.

[pause for questions]

Before transformers, models processed text sequentially, which made long range relationships hard to capture. Attention lets every word look at every other word at once, and this scales remarkably well. The more data and compute researchers used, the better these models got, with no obvious ceiling for a long time.

This scaling led to models with billions, then hundreds of billions of parameters, the internal numbers a model adjusts during training. A model learns by predicting the next word repeatedly across enormous text corpora, adjusting parameters each time it is wrong. Trained on much of the public internet plus books and code, these models develop broad abilities to write, summarize, translate, and answer questions.

[slide transition]

But there is a fundamental limitation. A language model only knows what was in its training data, which has a cutoff date, and it cannot know anything private or specific to your own documents. Crucially, when a model does not know something, it often does not say so, instead generating a plausible but false answer. This is called hallucination, one of the biggest problems in deploying these systems.

[pause for questions]

Retrieval augmented generation, or RAG, combines language models with a search step to solve exactly this problem. Instead of relying only on memorized knowledge, the system first retrieves relevant documents from an external source, then feeds those documents to the model along with the question, so the answer stays grounded in real, verifiable information.

[pause for questions]

A RAG pipeline has two phases. Indexing happens ahead of time: documents are loaded, split into chunks, embedded into vectors, and stored in a vector database. Retrieval and generation happen at question time: the question is embedded, the nearest chunks are retrieved, combined with the question into a prompt, and sent to the model to generate a grounded answer.

[pause for questions]

Document loading is the first indexing step, reading raw text out of sources like PDFs, web pages, transcripts, or wikis. Splitting comes next, because a document is often too long to embed or retrieve precisely as one piece, so it is broken into chunks of a few hundred to a thousand characters, usually with some overlap so sentences are not awkwardly cut at chunk boundaries.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

Embedding converts each chunk into a vector, a list of numbers capturing its meaning, so that texts with similar meaning end up close together in vector space, even if they use completely different words. This is what allows meaning based search rather than exact keyword matching.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

Storing places these vectors, alongside the original text, into a vector database such as FAISS, Chroma, or Pinecone. These databases are optimized for one operation: given a query vector, quickly find the most similar stored vectors, even across millions of chunks.

At question time, the user's query is embedded the same way, and the vector store returns the closest matching chunks, typically the top three to five. These retrieved chunks are combined with the question into an augmented prompt, usually with an instruction telling the model to answer only from the provided context and to say when it does not know.

Generation is the final step: the augmented prompt, containing both retrieved context and the question, goes to the language model, which produces an answer grounded in the retrieved material rather than its own memory, making the answer more accurate and able to reflect information the model never saw during training.

[pause for questions]

Maximal marginal relevance, or MMR, is a retrieval refinement. Plain similarity search can return several nearly identical chunks, wasting space and narrowing the model's view. MMR selects chunks that are both relevant and diverse from each other, improving coverage of the topic.

The multi query retriever is another refinement. A single phrasing of a question searches from only one angle, and documents phrased differently might be missed. This approach uses a language model to rewrite the question several ways, searches with each version, and merges the unique results, catching more relevant material.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

Contextual compression is a third refinement. A retrieved chunk might contain one relevant sentence buried in an otherwise irrelevant paragraph. A compressor model extracts just the relevant portion of each chunk before it reaches the final model, keeping context clean and reducing cost.

A natural question is why not simply make the context window bigger and include everything. Even with large context windows, processing huge context for every question is slow and expensive, models attend less well to information buried in the middle of very long input, and knowledge bases can be far larger than any context window could hold. Retrieval remains the practical solution.

[slide transition]

RAG does not eliminate hallucination entirely, it reduces it substantially. A model can still misread retrieved context, or retrieval can fetch the wrong chunks, so good chunking, good retrieval, and clear prompts all matter. RAG is also not limited to question answering, the same pattern underlies summarization, grounded drafting, customer support, and research assistants.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

Evaluating a RAG system means measuring two things separately: retrieval quality, whether the right chunks are fetched for a given question, and answer quality, whether the final answer is accurate, complete, and faithful to the retrieved source. Faithfulness matters especially, since an answer can sound convincing while contradicting its own retrieved context.

[slide transition]

In production, indexing often runs as a background job triggered whenever new documents arrive, keeping the vector store synchronized with the source of truth. Updates and deletions must be handled carefully, since stale entries in the vector store lead directly to wrong answers later.

Cost and latency are constant engineering concerns. Every embedding call and every generation call costs time and money. Caching common questions, batching embedding requests, and matching model size to each step's difficulty are standard optimizations; not every step needs the largest available model.

Retrieval augmented models turn a general purpose language model into a specialist on your own information. A hospital can ground an assistant in its medical protocols, a law firm in its case files, a software company in its documentation. The underlying model stays the same; the retrieved knowledge is what makes it useful for that specific domain.

[brief technical difficulty, resuming]

For decades, answering questions about your own data meant building rigid systems with hand written rules and structured databases. Now, pointing a retrieval augmented model at a pile of unstructured documents can produce a system answering natural language questions, with citations, in a matter of hours rather than months.

[slide transition]

Retrieval is not perfect. Embeddings capture meaning imperfectly, and the chunk that looks most similar by vector distance is not always the most useful one. Active research areas include hybrid search that combines keyword and vector search, re ranking steps using a second model to reorder retrieved candidates, and methods letting the model decide for itself when and what to retrieve.

[audience laughter]

Trust and verification matter alongside retrieval. Good RAG systems provide citations linking each part of an answer back to its source chunk, letting a human verify the claim, which is essential in high stakes domains like medicine, law, and finance.

For a first retrieval augmented system, start simple: a handful of documents, reasonable chunking, straightforward embedding, a simple vector store, and a basic retrieve then generate pipeline, working end to end before adding refinements. Measure where the simple version falls short, then add MMR, multi query, or compression only where they demonstrably help.

The quality of a retrieval augmented system is bounded by the quality of the documents feeding it. Cleaning documents, removing duplicates, fixing formatting, and chunking thoughtfully often improves results more than switching to a fancier model.

The prompt wrapping retrieved context matters more than people expect. A good prompt clearly instructs the model to rely on the provided context, to say when it does not know, and to avoid inventing information. Small changes in phrasing can noticeably change how faithful and honest the resulting answers are.

To summarize so far: artificial intelligence evolved from hand written rules to systems that learn from data. Deep learning and the transformer architecture unlocked powerful language models. Those models are powerful but limited by training data and prone to hallucination. Retrieval augmented generation grounds the model in retrieved documents, following the pipeline of load, split, embed, store, retrieve, augment, and generate.

Let's go deeper on tokenization, since it underlies everything a language model does. Text is broken into tokens, which might be whole words, parts of words, or punctuation. A tokenizer trained on a large corpus builds a vocabulary of common subword pieces, so rare words can still be represented as a sequence of familiar fragments rather than an unknown symbol. Token count, not word count, is what determines cost and context window usage in practice.

[slide transition]

Positional information also matters in transformers. Because attention looks at all tokens at once rather than in strict order, the model needs some signal about where each token sits in the sequence. Positional encodings, whether fixed sinusoidal patterns or learned embeddings, are added to each token so the model can distinguish the first word of a sentence from the last.

[pause for questions]

Fine tuning is a technique for adapting a pretrained model to a narrower task by continuing training on a smaller, more specific dataset. This differs from retrieval augmented generation: fine tuning changes the model's internal weights permanently, while RAG leaves the model unchanged and instead supplies fresh information at query time through the prompt. The two approaches are complementary rather than competing.

[audience laughter]

Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback are two techniques that shape how a base language model behaves as an assistant. A base model, trained purely to predict next tokens, is not naturally good at following instructions or refusing harmful requests. Additional training stages teach the model to be helpful, honest, and safe in conversation, which is why a raw pretrained model and a released chat assistant can feel very different to use.

[audience laughter]

Vector similarity itself deserves attention. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their magnitude, which works well when embedding magnitude carries little meaningful information. Euclidean distance instead measures straight line distance and can behave differently depending on how the embedding model was trained. Most vector databases let you choose the distance metric, and the right choice can meaningfully affect retrieval quality.

Chunk size is one of the most impactful and most overlooked design decisions in a RAG system. Chunks that are too small lose surrounding context, so a retrieved sentence can be technically relevant but impossible to interpret correctly on its own. Chunks that are too large dilute the embedding, since a long chunk covering several topics produces a vector that is a blurry average of all of them, making it harder for a query to match precisely.

[audience laughter]

Metadata filtering is a powerful complement to pure vector search. By attaching structured tags to each chunk, such as a source document, a date, an author, or a category, a retrieval system can narrow the search space before or alongside the similarity ranking. This is especially useful when a knowledge base spans many distinct sources and a user's question implies which subset is relevant.

[pause for questions]

Hybrid search combines traditional keyword based search, such as BM25, with vector similarity search. Keyword search excels at exact matches, like a specific product code or an exact phrase, cases where semantic similarity search can sometimes miss the mark because the embedding space does not preserve exact string matches well. Combining both signals, often with a weighted blend or a re ranking step, tends to outperform either approach alone.

[slide transition]

Re ranking is a two stage retrieval strategy. A fast, approximate method, usually vector similarity search, first pulls a larger candidate set of documents, perhaps twenty or fifty chunks. Then a slower, more accurate model scores each candidate specifically against the query and reorders them, keeping only the very best few for the final prompt. This trades a bit of extra computation for meaningfully better precision.

Agents extend the RAG idea further, giving a language model the ability to take actions and decide for itself what to retrieve or which tool to call, rather than following one fixed retrieve then generate pipeline. An agent might decide a question needs a web search, a database query, and a calculator, chaining several retrieval and computation steps together before producing a final answer.

[pause for questions]

Multimodal retrieval extends these ideas beyond text, embedding images, audio, or video alongside text descriptions into a shared vector space, so a text query can retrieve a relevant image, or an image query can retrieve relevant text. This is increasingly important as knowledge bases include diagrams, screenshots, and recorded meetings alongside written documents.

[slide transition]

Latency budgets shape real world RAG architecture more than people expect. A user expects a response within a few seconds, which constrains how many retrieval rounds, re ranking passes, and generation tokens a system can afford. Production systems often cache embeddings for frequently accessed documents and precompute retrieval for common queries to stay within that budget.

[pause for questions]

Security deserves particular attention in retrieval augmented systems that operate over private data. If a vector store mixes documents from different users or permission levels, a poorly designed retriever can leak information a user should never see. Filtering by access control at retrieval time, not just at the application layer, is essential for any system handling sensitive or multi tenant data.

As models and retrieval systems keep improving, the boundary between what a model knows innately and what it needs to look up will keep shifting, but the core idea will likely remain: separate the reasoning ability of a language model from the storage of facts, and let retrieval bridge the two. That separation is what makes these systems both more accurate and easier to keep up to date.

Re ranking is a two stage retrieval strategy. A fast, approximate method, usually vector similarity search, first pulls a larger candidate set of documents, perhaps twenty or fifty chunks. Then a slower, more accurate model scores each candidate specifically against the query and reorders them, keeping only the very best few for the final prompt. This trades a bit of extra computation for meaningfully better precision.
